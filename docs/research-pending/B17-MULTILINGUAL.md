## WHY THIS MATTERS

The B1-B16 research is English-centric. The /s/ brightness finding is English-specific. The formant norms (Hillenbrand 1995) are US English. The "feminine-coded patterns" assume English prosody. But the app will have multilingual users — many trans women speak Spanish, Mandarin, Japanese, Arabic, German, Hindi, or other languages as their primary or only language.

Worse, **tonal languages** (Mandarin, Vietnamese, Thai, Cantonese, etc.) make some English voice training principles literally impossible — you can't "raise your pitch" without changing the words' meanings.

This branch covers the major considerations for non-English learners and the principles for adapting the curriculum.

## SECTION 1 — WHAT TRANSFERS FROM ENGLISH VOICE TRAINING

[VERIFIED — universal biomechanics; applies to all languages]

These qualities are LANGUAGE-INDEPENDENT and apply to MtF voice training in any language:

1. **Higher speaking pitch** (within the limits of the language) — pitch elevation works in all languages; the target zone varies
2. **Forward buzz** (tongue forward, lips gently spread, brighter mouth resonance) — this is pure vocal tract shaping, independent of language
3. **Lighter voice** (less chest rumble, lighter voice-box edge contact) — biomechanical, language-independent
4. **Slight breathiness** — language-independent
5. **Body foundations** (breath, posture, throat release) — pure body mechanics

These five qualities form the universal foundation. They transfer to every language.

## SECTION 2 — WHAT DOESN'T TRANSFER

[VERIFIED — language-specific features]

### 2.1 The /s/ brightness cue
[VERIFIED for English; varies across languages]

The /s/ brightness finding (B5) is most-studied in English. The exact frequency, the exact tongue position, the exact gender difference magnitude all vary across languages:

- **English /s/**: center of gravity ~6-9 kHz feminine, ~4-7 kHz masculine
- **Spanish /s/**: often pronounced with a different tongue position; in some dialects, aspirated or dropped entirely (Caribbean Spanish); in Castilian, "distinción" or "ceceo" affects the /s/ vs /θ/ distinction
- **Japanese /s/**: tends to be lighter and slightly different in tongue position
- **Slavic /s/**: often retroflex or darker
- **Arabic /s/**: varies by dialect

For MtF learners in non-English languages: the /s/ may or may not be the most gendered consonant. The biomechanical principle (forward tongue, spread lips = brighter hiss) still applies, but the specific target sound may differ.

### 2.2 Intonation patterns
Each language has its own intonation patterns. The English "terminal lift" (B5 §7) is one pattern; not universal:

- **English**: feminine-coded = rising or level terminals; masculine = falling terminals
- **Spanish**: different patterns; often wider pitch swings in both genders
- **Japanese**: complex pitch-accent + sentence-final particles carry gender (see Section 4)
- **Mandarin**: tone language — pitch is constrained by word meaning (see Section 5)
- **French**: relatively flat intonation within phrases; different gender cues
- **German**: more pitch variation than English in formal speech

### 2.3 Formant norms
The female-male formant differences (Hillenbrand 1995) are US English data. Other languages may show:
- Different absolute formant values (due to different average vocal tract lengths in different populations)
- Different female-male ratios
- Different perceptual weight given to different formants

### 2.4 Speech rate and rhythm
[VERIFIED — Jacewicz 2009 from B5]

"Women speak faster" is FALSE for English. The pattern differs across languages:
- Some languages show no sex difference in rate
- Some show women slightly faster
- Some show men slightly faster
- Don't apply English-specific rate findings to other languages

## SECTION 3 — SPANISH-SPEAKING LEARNERS

[VERIFIED for general Spanish phonetics; PEDAGOGICAL CONVENTION for trans-specific application since formal MtF Spanish voice research is thin]

### Spanish-specific features that matter for MtF

**Phonology:**
- 5-vowel system (/a/ /e/ /i/ /o/ /u/) vs English's 11+ vowels — vowel training is simpler but vowel targets differ
- Syllable-timed rhythm (vs English stress-timed) — each syllable gets more equal time
- /s/ often aspirated (becomes /h/) in many dialects (Caribbean, coastal Latin America, southern Spain) — the /s/ brightness cue may not apply
- /r/ is tapped or trilled — different from English /r/
- /θ/ "ceceo" vs /s/ "seseo" distinction in Castilian Spanish

**Intonation:**
- Wider pitch swings than English
- Both genders use more pitch variation
- Feminine-coded: tends toward even wider swings
- Question intonation often rises throughout, not just at end

**Vocal fry:**
- Less common than in English
- More stigmatized in formal Spanish

### What's the same as English
- Higher pitch target
- Forward buzz
- Lighter voice
- Body foundations

### What's different
- /s/ work less central (especially in aspirated-s dialects)
- Vowel work different (5 vowels vs 11+)
- Rhythm different (syllable-timed)
- Intonation patterns different

### Curriculum adaptation for Spanish-speaking learners
- Substitute /s/ drill with vowel drill emphasis (the 5 Spanish vowels)
- Substitute rhythm work with syllable-timed pattern
- Use Spanish-language reference voices, not English
- Intonation drills use Spanish patterns
- All micro-trigger cues in Spanish

## SECTION 4 — JAPANESE-SPEAKING LEARNERS

[VERIFIED — extensive research on gendered Japanese speech]

### Japanese-specific features

**Pitch accent system:**
Japanese is a pitch-accent language — pitch patterns distinguish word meanings (e.g., hashi = "bridge" or "chopsticks" depending on pitch pattern). This constrains pitch-target training.

**Onnarashii speech / joseigo (women's language):**
[VERIFIED — Wikipedia, Bondlingo, Academia.edu]
- Higher pitch (universal)
- Longer vowel duration
- Rising intonation at phrase ends
- Wider pitch range
- Specific sentence-final particles: わ (wa), の (no), かしら (kashira), わよ (wa yo)
- More polite forms (keigo) expected
- Softer articulation
- More hesitation markers (あの, その)
- Specific first-person pronouns: わたし (watashi), あたし (atashi)

**Important: gendered Japanese speech is changing**
[VERIFIED — Meguro English, TV Tropes]
- Younger Japanese women use less joseigo
- Many younger women use forms once coded masculine
- Rigid feminine speech can sound "old-fashioned or performed"
- Anime/drama characters still use exaggerated joseigo (the "Coconut Effect")

### What's different for MtF Japanese learners
- Pitch elevation is CONSTRAINED by pitch-accent system — you can't just "raise your pitch" because that changes word meanings
- The target is to use the SAME pitch-accent patterns but in a higher REGISTER
- Sentence-final particles carry major gender weight — learning these is essential
- Politeness level (keigo) interacts with gender

### Curriculum adaptation
- Pitch work must respect pitch-accent integrity
- Sentence-final particle training (Japanese-specific)
- Pitch REGISTER elevation (higher overall) instead of intonation changes
- Vowel duration work (feminine-coded in Japanese)
- Reference voices: Japanese women, not English speakers

## SECTION 5 — MANDARIN/CANTONESE LEARNERS (tonal languages)

[VERIFIED — Mandarin phonology; PEDAGOGICAL CONVENTION for trans-specific]

### The critical complication

Mandarin has 4 lexical tones (5 including neutral):
- Tone 1: high level
- Tone 2: rising
- Tone 3: low (often with creaky voice)
- Tone 4: falling
- Neutral tone: short, unstressed

**Each tone must be pronounced correctly or the word meaning changes.** This means:
- You CANNOT simply "raise your pitch" — that breaks tones
- The feminine-coded English pattern of "wider pitch swings" is constrained by tone integrity
- The terminal lift pattern must work within the tone system

### How feminine voice works in tonal languages
The feminine cues in Mandarin come from:
1. **Higher register** — the overall pitch range is shifted up while maintaining relative tone relationships (Tone 1 is still highest, Tone 3 is still lowest, but everything is in a higher absolute range)
2. **Breathiness** — language-independent
3. **Forward buzz** — language-independent
4. **Lighter voice** — language-independent
5. **Specific particles and vocabulary** (Mandarin has some gendered sentence-final particles, less than Japanese)
6. **Softer articulation**

### What's HARDER for Mandarin-speaking MtF learners
- Cannot use the English pattern of "wider pitch swings within phrases" — the tone system already specifies the pitch contour of each syllable
- The main lever is REGISTER shift (everything higher), not intonation change
- This requires finer pitch control, not broader pitch range

### Cantonese-specific
- 6-9 tones (depending on analysis) — even more constrained than Mandarin
- Same principles: register shift + breathiness + forward buzz

### Curriculum adaptation
- Pitch REGISTER work (raising overall baseline while preserving tone relationships)
- Tone-preserved drills (sustain tone 1, tone 2, etc. in higher register)
- Less emphasis on intonation work (constrained by tone system)
- Reference voices: Mandarin/Cantonese women
- Micro-trigger cues in Mandarin/Cantonese

## SECTION 7 — MULTILINGUAL LEARNERS (code-switching)

[VERIFIED — bilingualism research]

### Many trans women speak multiple languages
- Heritage speakers (Spanish at home, English at school)
- Immigrants (native language + new country's language)
- International multilinguals (English + regional + others)

### Each language may have its own voice pattern
- The learner may have a feminine English voice but a masculine Spanish voice
- Or vice versa
- Code-switching between languages often triggers code-switching between voice patterns

### Curriculum considerations
- Identify which language(s) the learner wants to feminize
- Train each language separately if multiple
- Each language has its own reference voices, drills, and targets
- The universal biomechanics transfer; the language-specific features don't

## SECTION 8 — APP DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

### Multi-language support
- App should support multiple primary languages
- Onboarding asks: "What language(s) do you want to feminize your voice in?"
- Each language has its own:
  - Reference voice library
  - Drill content
  - Intonation patterns
  - Specific gender cues
  - Micro-trigger cues translated

### Tonal language adaptation
- For Mandarin/Cantonese/Vietnamese/Thai learners, the app MUST respect tone integrity
- Pitch drills preserve tone relationships
- Different curriculum emphasis (register shift vs intonation)

### Spanish/Portuguese/French adaptation
- Substitute vowel system
- Adjust rhythm work (syllable-timed)
- Adjust /s/ work (especially for aspirated-s dialects)

### Japanese adaptation
- Pitch REGISTER work (within pitch-accent system)
- Sentence-final particle training
- Specific joseigo/onnarashii elements

### Arabic/Hindi/other adaptation
- Per-language phonetic work
- Specific consonant training
- Different intonation patterns

### Code-switching support
- For multilingual learners: separate voice profiles per language
- "Practice in English" vs "Practice in Spanish" modes
- Cross-language reinforcement (body/breath work transfers; language features don't)

### Reference voice library expansion
- Beyond English voices (the P0 ship-blocker from the audit)
- Voices in Spanish, Mandarin, Japanese, Arabic, etc.
- Regional variation within each language

## SECTION 9 — THE UNIVERSAL CURRICULUM ADAPTATION PRINCIPLE

### Layer 1 (universal, language-independent)
Body foundations, breath, posture, throat release, buzz location awareness, lighter voice, breathiness — these apply to every language and are taught the same way.

### Layer 2 (mostly universal, minor variation)
Higher pitch register (within the language's tolerance for pitch movement) — applies everywhere but the implementation varies for tonal languages.

### Layer 3 (language-specific)
Intonation patterns, terminal pitch, specific consonant cues (/s/ for English, particles for Japanese, etc.), rhythm patterns, vowel systems — must be adapted per language.

### Layer 4 (cultural/regional)
Specific reference voices, regional accent preservation, subcultural voice patterns (B15) — varies by culture and individual.

The app should make Layers 1-2 universal, Layer 3 language-specific, and Layer 4 personalized.
