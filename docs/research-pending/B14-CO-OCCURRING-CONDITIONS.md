## WHY THIS MATTERS

The core TransVoice pedagogy (B1-B13) assumes:
- Intact interoception (you can feel your body)
- Intact proprioception (you can sense where your tongue/jaw/larynx are)
- Adequate breath capacity
- Adequate hearing for self-monitoring
- Stable connective tissue
- Ability to sustain daily practice habits
- A regulated nervous system (low baseline anxiety)

Many trans learners have one or more conditions that violate these assumptions. Without accessibility adaptations, the curriculum fails them — they blame themselves, not the curriculum. This branch documents the major co-occurring conditions and how to adapt.

**The principle:** adapt the pedagogy to the learner, not the learner to the pedagogy.

## SECTION 1 — AUTISM SPECTRUM & INTEROCEPTION/PROPRIOCEPTION DIFFERENCES

[VERIFIED — autism research consensus on sensory differences; PEDAGOGICAL CONVENTION for specific adaptations]

### What's different
Autistic learners commonly have:
- **Atypical interoception** — difficulty feeling internal body signals (breath location, belly movement, throat tension)
- **Atypical proprioception** — difficulty sensing where the tongue, jaw, and larynx are in space
- **Sensory hypersensitivity or hyposensitivity** — body sensations may be overwhelming or barely registered
- **Motor planning differences** — sequencing complex multi-step movements (like coordinating breath + tongue + jaw + larynx) may be harder
- **Routine dependency** — practices that fit a rigid routine work better than flexible practices

### Why this matters for sensation-based voice work
The ENTIRE B7 pedagogy is built on "feel where the buzz is." If interoception is atypical, the learner may genuinely not be able to feel whether the buzz is in the chest or the face. This is NOT a failure of effort — it's a sensory access issue.

### Adaptations
1. **Use external feedback instead of internal sensation.** Mirrors, video, audio recording, biofeedback devices (when available). Don't rely on "feel where the buzz is."
2. **Make sensation concrete.** Instead of "feel the buzz in your face," try "place your fingers on your cheekbones; do you feel them vibrate?" Externalize the internal.
3. **Slow down the curriculum.** Phase 1 body awareness (B12) may take 4-6 weeks instead of 2. Allow this.
4. **Chunk smaller.** Break each drill into smaller steps with explicit check-ins between.
5. **Use visual aids.** Diagrams of the voice box, tongue positions (side-profile drawings), posture photos. Many autistic learners are visual thinkers.
6. **Build a rigid routine.** Same time, same place, same order, every day. Predictability enables consistency.
7. **Be explicit about what to feel.** Don't say "you'll know it when you feel it." Say "you should feel a tickle on the inside of your upper lip."
8. **Consider working with an occupational therapist** for interoception building exercises alongside voice work.

### Specifically challenging drills
- "Where do you feel the buzz?" (B7 §1.4 body scan) — may need to be replaced with "put your fingers here; do you feel vibration?"
- "Throat soft or tight?" — may not be discernible; replace with "look in the mirror; is your Adam's apple moving?"
- Sustained attention on subtle sensations — may need shorter drills (3 min instead of 10) with breaks

## SECTION 2 — ADHD & EXECUTIVE FUNCTION DIFFERENCES

[VERIFIED — ADHD research consensus on executive function; PEDAGOGICAL CONVENTION for adaptations]

### What's different
- **Difficulty sustaining daily habits** without external structure
- **Time blindness** — 15 min can feel like 5 or 50
- **Difficulty with delayed gratification** — the payoff is months away
- **Novelty seeking** — repetitive drills feel intolerable
- **Working memory limitations** — multi-step instructions may be lost

### Adaptations
1. **External structure is non-negotiable.** App notifications, accountability partner, calendar block, gamification. The internal structure won't hold.
2. **Short sessions, high frequency.** 5 min × 3 times/day often beats 15 min × 1/day. Smaller commitment, easier to start.
3. **Gamify.** Streaks, badges, levels, progress bars. Dopamine rewards help bridge the motivation gap.
4. **Body-doubling.** Practice with a friend (in person or on video call) or with a "study with me" YouTube stream.
5. **Vary the drills.** Don't do the exact same 10-min sequence every day. Rotate drills; keep the body learning but the brain engaged.
6. **Make starting absurdly easy.** "I'll just do the body scan" often turns into a full session. Reduce activation energy.
7. **Accept inconsistency.** ADHD learners may have peaks (hyperfocus weeks) and valleys (executive dysfunction weeks). The trend line matters more than any single day.
8. **Visible progress tracking.** Calendar X's, pitch-over-time graph, before/after recordings. Tangible evidence of progress sustains motivation.

## SECTION 3 — LARYNGOPHARYNGEAL REFLUX (LPR / silent reflux)

[VERIFIED — ENT/voice pathology consensus on LPR]

### What LPR is
Stomach acid travels up the esophagus and into the throat, irritating the vocal folds. Often "silent" — no heartburn, just throat symptoms. Extremely common; often undiagnosed.

### Symptoms (voice-relevant)
- Chronic throat clearing (which damages folds — B6)
- Morning hoarseness
- Feeling of "something stuck" in throat (globus)
- Chronic cough
- Voice fatigue
- Excess mucus in throat

### Why LPR sabotages voice training
- The acid irritates the folds, making them swell
- Swollen folds vibrate less efficiently → voice sounds deeper, harder to feminize
- The irritation triggers throat-clearing (B6 §6) → more damage
- Breath support work may aggravate reflux (increased intra-abdominal pressure)
- Aspirate onsets (B7 §2.4) may be harder with irritated folds

### What to do
1. **Diagnose and treat LPR FIRST or CONCURRENTLY.** Voice training on irritated folds is like running on a sprained ankle. See an ENT or gastroenterologist.
2. **Standard LPR management:**
   - Don't eat 3 hours before bed
   - Raise the head of the bed 6 inches
   - Avoid: caffeine, alcohol, spicy food, fatty food, chocolate, peppermint, citrus, tomatoes
   - PPI medication (omeprazole etc.) as prescribed
   - Lose weight if relevant
   - Stop smoking
3. **Adapt voice practice around LPR:**
   - Practice mid-day, not morning (morning voice is most reflux-affected)
   - Hydrate heavily
   - Sip water during practice
   - Avoid throat-clearing before/during practice
4. **Recovery timeline:** LPR control takes 4-8 weeks of consistent management. Voice practice can continue during this time but expect setbacks.

## SECTION 4 — ASTHMA

[VERIFIED — respiratory therapy consensus]

### What's different
- **Breath support work** (B4) is harder — the airways themselves are reactive
- **Inhaler use** can affect voice (corticosteroid inhalers can cause oral thrush or fold irritation)
- **Asthma attacks** interrupt voice practice
- **Exercise-induced asthma** may flare during sustained phonation work
- **Anxiety about breath** can compound vocal tension

### Adaptations
1. **Have inhaler accessible during practice.** Always. Even for low-intensity work.
2. **Inhaler technique matters.** Rinse mouth after corticosteroid inhaler to prevent thrush.
3. **Practice in mid-breath range, not at breath extremes.** Don't deplete breath; manage the breath budget (B13 §8).
4. **Breathe through the nose when possible** (warms and humidifies air).
5. **Avoid practice during asthma flares.** Rest; come back when stable.
6. **Slower progression through Phase 1 (B12) body/breath work.** Building diaphragmatic breath takes longer with asthma.
7. **Coordinate with pulmonologist/asthma action plan.** Voice practice shouldn't conflict with medical management.
8. **Accept good days and bad days.** Asthma variability is normal; don't judge voice progress on a flare day.

### Specifically challenging drills
- Sustained phonation (10-sec "ah") may need to be shorter for asthma learners
- Lip trills require steady breath — may need breaks
- Sirens across full range may trigger coughing — start with smaller range

## SECTION 5 — HEARING IMPAIRMENT

[VERIFIED — Deaf/hard-of-hearing voice therapy literature]

### What's different
- **Self-monitoring is impaired.** The whole B8d real-time self-monitoring system (where's the buzz? did the sentence end up or down?) depends on hearing yourself.
- **Pitch matching is harder.** If you can't hear a target pitch, you can't match it.
- **Feedback loops are slower.** Cis-typical hearing learners adjust in real-time; HoH learners may need to record and review later.

### Adaptations
1. **Visual feedback is critical.** Pitch-tracking apps, spectrograms (when available), vibration sensors. Anything that converts audio to visual.
2. **Tactile feedback.** Place fingers on the voice box to feel pitch changes; place fingers on the chest/face to feel buzz location.
3. **Recording + review.** Shorter feedback loop than real-time, but still works.
4. **Sign language interpreters** for any clinician sessions.
5. **Work with a Deaf-aware SLP** if available.
6. **Captioning** for any video/audio content in the app (B7-style atlas should be text-based, not audio-only).
7. **Slow down.** HoH learners may need 1.5-2x the standard timeline.
8. **Vibrotactile aids** exist (wearable devices that translate sound to vibration) — may help with pitch awareness.

### Note
Many Deaf signers have rich, gendered signing styles that DON'T depend on voice at all. For some Deaf trans people, signing is the primary gender expression and voice work is secondary or irrelevant. Don't assume all trans people want voice work.

## SECTION 6 — JOINT HYPERMOBILITY (Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome, hEDS)

[VERIFIED — EDS clinical knowledge; PEDAGOGICAL CONVENTION for voice adaptations]

### What's different
- **Connective tissue is more elastic than typical.** Joints move beyond normal ranges. The larynx is stabilized by connective tissue.
- **Voice box may sit lower or move more freely** — can affect pitch and stability
- **TMJ (jaw joint) often affected** — jaw tension, clicking, subluxation
- **Easy bruising, fragile tissue** — vocal fold irritation from throat-clearing hits harder
- **POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome) common comorbidity** — affects posture, breath, blood flow

### Adaptations
1. **Gentle jaw work.** Don't push jaw stretches — risk of subluxation. Soft, gentle movements only.
2. **Postural modifications for POTS.** If standing causes dizziness, practice seated or reclined. Build up gradually.
3. **Slower voice box work.** The voice box may already be mobile; the work is more about coordination than mobility.
4. **Avoid extremes of any stretch.** Don't push the front-throat stretch to maximum — gentle engagement only.
5. **Compression garments** may help POTS symptoms during practice.
6. **Hydrate heavily** (helps connective tissue + blood volume for POTS).
7. **Work with a physical therapist** familiar with EDS for the postural foundation.
8. **Be patient.** Hypermobility can be a voice asset (more laryngeal mobility) but it takes longer to coordinate.

## SECTION 7 — ANXIETY DISORDERS

[VERIFIED — clinical psychology + voice therapy intersection]

### What's different
- **Anxiety directly causes throat tension.** The "lump in throat" (globus) sensation, the tight swallowing — these are sympathetic nervous system responses.
- **Practice avoidance.** Anxiety makes practice feel threatening; learners skip days.
- **Performance anxiety in real contexts.** Phone calls, social interactions, work meetings become flooded with anxiety, which collapses technique (B13).
- **Recording anxiety.** Listening to your own voice is dysphoria-triggering for many trans people; anxiety compounds this.

### Adaptations
1. **Start with body regulation, not voice.** Before any voice practice: 2-5 min of grounding (deep breaths, body scan, sensory awareness). A regulated nervous system enables motor learning.
2. **Tiny commitments.** "Just 2 minutes" reduces activation energy. Often leads to longer sessions once started.
3. **Trauma-informed approach.** For some trans people, the throat is a site of dysphoria, bullying, or trauma. Voice work can be triggering. Move slowly; consent to each new drill.
4. **Working with a gender-affirming therapist** alongside voice work is often essential for anxious learners.
5. **Reduce recording frequency.** If recording spikes anxiety, do it monthly, not weekly. Focus on sensation over audio feedback.
6. **Pre-context grounding.** Before phone calls or social events: 30 sec of slow breathing. This helps more than technique review.
7. **Accept that anxiety will degrade technique in real contexts** and that's OK. Recovery is the skill.

## SECTION 8 — DEPRESSION

[VERIFIED — depression research consensus on motivation/energy]

### What's different
- **Practice consistency is hard.** Depression saps motivation, energy, executive function.
- **Voice itself may sound flat.** Depression reduces pitch range, intonation variability, vocal energy — exactly the opposite of the feminine-coded patterns (B5).
- **Self-worth attacks.** "I'll never pass. Why bother." Predictable depression thoughts.
- **Fatigue.** Mental and physical.

### Adaptations
1. **Lower the bar.** 5 min of practice is better than 0. Don't aim for 30 min; aim for "any practice today."
2. **Mechanical practice is fine.** On bad days, you don't have to feel hopeful or motivated. Just do the body scan and one drill. Emotion follows action.
3. **Build practice into existing routines.** "After I brush my teeth" is more reliable than "I'll find time today."
4. **Treat depression first or concurrently.** Therapy, medication, support. Voice work is harder when depression is untreated.
5. **Accept flat-voice days.** The technique will return when energy returns.
6. **Celebrate small wins aggressively.** "I did 5 min on a bad day" deserves real acknowledgment.

## SECTION 9 — PTSD & TRAUMA

[VERIFIED — trauma-informed care consensus; PEDAGOGICAL CONVENTION for voice-specific adaptations]

### What's different
- **Throat as trauma site.** Many trans people (especially those who've experienced choking, strangulation, medical trauma, or voice-related bullying) have specific trauma held in the throat.
- **Voice practice can trigger.** The attention on throat, breath, voice can surface traumatic memories or body memories.
- **Dissociation.** Trauma survivors may dissociate during body-focused work.
- **Hyperarousal.** Practice can trigger fight-or-flight, making motor learning impossible.

### Adaptations
1. **Trauma-informed pacing.** Go slower than the standard curriculum. Consent to each new drill. Pause on request.
2. **Grounding before voice work.** Always start with grounding (5 senses, feet on floor, present-moment awareness).
3. **Don't push through discomfort.** If a drill triggers, STOP. Note it. Come back later, gently.
4. **Choice and control.** The learner chooses the drill, the duration, the order. Autonomy is medicine for trauma.
5. **Work with a trauma therapist** alongside voice work. Often essential.
6. **Body scan modifications.** For trauma survivors, the standard body scan may need to be modified (e.g., skip throat focus if it triggers; start with hands or feet).
7. **The voice teacher matters.** Trauma-informed voice teachers exist; they're worth finding.

## SECTION 10 — PRE-EXISTING VOICE PATHOLOGY

[VERIFIED — ENT/SLP clinical consensus]

### If learner has vocal fold nodules
- Nodules are calluses from chronic friction (B6 §9)
- Voice training BEFORE treating nodules will make them worse
- Standard treatment: voice therapy to remove the friction behavior, then nodules shrink
- For trans learners: work with a gender-affirming SLP who can address BOTH the nodule-causing behaviors AND the feminization goals
- Surgery is rarely needed; behavioral treatment usually resolves nodules in 2-6 months

### If learner has muscle tension dysphonia (MTD)
- MTD = throat muscles over-grip during phonation
- Common in trans learners who learned to "force" a higher voice via squeezing (B3 §6)
- Treatment: voice therapy to release the extrinsic tension and re-coordinate the intrinsic muscles
- The B7 pedagogy is largely MTD-preventive IF the learner follows the "throat stays soft" rule
- For learners with existing MTD: Phase 1 (body awareness) may take 4-8 weeks; focus on release before any quality work

### If learner has spasmodic dysphonia
- Neurological voice disorder; voice breaks/spasms involuntarily
- Specialized treatment (Botox injections, voice therapy)
- Voice feminization work can be done concurrently but is more complex
- Requires specialist SLP

### If learner has vocal fold polyps or cysts
- Structural lesions; may need surgical removal
- Voice therapy alone usually insufficient
- See ENT first; address lesion, then proceed with feminization

### If learner has had previous voice surgery (e.g., CTA, glottoplasty)
- See B11 (surgery rehab) — different starting point
- May have surgical complications to work around
- Voice therapy essential to optimize outcome

## SECTION 11 — ACCESSIBILITY WRAP-UP

### Universal design principles for the app
1. **Multiple modalities for every concept.** Text + visual + audio + tactile cue. Different learners access differently.
2. **Adjustable pacing.** Some learners need 36 weeks for the standard curriculum; some need 12. Let them adjust.
3. **Self-rated vs audio-analyzed assessment.** Some learners can't reliably self-rate (autism, interoception issues); some can't audio-monitor (HoH). Offer both.
4. **Granular settings.** "I have X condition" → curriculum adapts (slower pace, modified drills, different modalities).
5. **Trauma-aware defaults.** Gentle, consent-based, never pushy. The learner is in control.
6. **Mental health integration.** App should acknowledge and link to mental health resources, not just voice content.
7. **Rest days are built-in.** No streak-shaming for taking a day off. Rest is part of practice.
8. **Multiple goal options.** Not every learner wants to pass as cis. Some want androgyny, some want singing, some want non-binary voice. Goals vary.

### The accessibility matrix

| Condition | Phase adjustment | Key adaptations | When to defer to specialist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autism | Phase 1 extended 4-6 wk | External feedback, visual aids, routine, chunking | OT for interoception |
| ADHD | Standard pace, high structure | External structure, gamification, body-doubling, micro-sessions | Med management if relevant |
| LPR | Treat concurrently | Mid-day practice, hydration, no throat-clear | GI/ENT |
| Asthma | Phase 1 extended | Inhaler accessible, smaller breath ranges, slower builds | Pulmonologist |
| HoH | 1.5-2x pace | Visual/tactile feedback, recording+review, slower | Deaf-aware SLP |
| EDS/hypermobility | Slower, gentler | No extreme stretches, posture mods, PT coordination | PT familiar with EDS |
| Anxiety | Phase 1 extended | Grounding first, tiny commitments, trauma-aware | Gender-affirming therapist |
| Depression | Standard pace, lower bar | Lower minimums, mechanical practice OK, existing routines | Therapy/meds |
| PTSD | Slower, consent-based | Grounding, choice, trauma-informed teacher | Trauma therapist |
| Pre-existing voice pathology | Treat pathology FIRST | None — defer to specialist | ENT + SLP |
