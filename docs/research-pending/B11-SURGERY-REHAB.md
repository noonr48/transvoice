## WHY THIS MATTERS

Voice feminization surgery (most commonly Wendler glottoplasty) is the only permanent way to raise the SPEAKING PITCH of the voice by altering the vocal source. But surgery alone is NOT sufficient — post-op voice therapy is required for ~6-12 months afterward to (a) recover from the surgical trauma, (b) learn to use the new instrument, (c) preserve the pitch gain long-term, and (d) address resonance/intonation/articulation that surgery can't change.

This branch documents the recovery timeline, the warning signs, and the rehab protocol — what the LEARNER experiences, what they should do, and what they should never do.

**Note on scope:** This entry covers rehab, NOT the surgery decision itself. For the decision (pros/cons/alternatives/surgeon selection), see B6 entry 2bd95ece and B9 entry (surgery warnings for singers).

## SECTION 1 — THE SURGERIES (brief recap)

### Wendler glottoplasty (most common)
Stitches the front of the vocal folds together to create a small web of scar tissue, which SHORTENS the vibrating length of the folds → raises pitch. Minimally invasive (endoscopic, through the mouth). Local or general anesthetic. Most widely performed.

### Laser-assisted glottoplasty / laser reduction
Uses a laser to thin or remove tissue at the front of the folds, achieving similar shortening. Less mechanical trauma than sutures.

### Cricothyroid approximation (CTA)
Older technique: permanently pulls the cricothyroid cartilages together to stretch the folds. Less favored now due to higher relapse rate and complications. Most surgeons prefer Wendler.

### Feminization laryngoplasty (Type 4 thyroplasty, Haber-style)
More invasive; reconstructs the laryngeal framework. Rare. Higher risk.

This entry focuses on **Wendler glottoplasty** recovery since it's by far the most common. Laser and CTA have similar but not identical protocols — always defer to the surgeon.

## SECTION 2 — PRE-OP REQUIREMENTS (what surgeons require before operating)

[VERIFIED — U Washington Gender-Affirming Voice Surgery program, LVS Clinic, Johns Hopkins]

Most reputable surgeons require:
1. **Completion of 6-12 months of voice therapy FIRST** — surgery is not a shortcut; therapy alone is sufficient for many trans women
2. **Informed consent documentation** — explicit understanding that surgery has risks and cannot restore voice if damaged
3. **Letters from mental health professionals** (some surgeons; WPATH-aligned)
4. **Vocal baseline assessment** — pre-op pitch, range, voice quality recordings
5. **Realistic expectations** — surgery raises SPEAKING PITCH typically +30-60 Hz; does NOT change resonance, intonation, articulation, or pass perception by itself
6. **For singers: explicit warning** that surgery often reduces singing range and quality (see B9 §13)

## SECTION 3 — RECOVERY TIMELINE (week by week, plain English)

### Pre-op day
- Pre-op voice recordings (baseline pitch, range)
- Stop blood-thinning meds per surgeon
- Arrange voice rest support (no work requiring voice, family/friends aware)

### Surgery day
- General or local anesthetic
- Endoscopic procedure (no external neck incision for Wendler)
- Recovery room: 1-3 hours observation
- Some patients discharged same day; others overnight
- Throat soreness, mild swelling, hoarse whisper if any sound at all

### Days 1-14: STRICT VOICE REST (some surgeons extend to 21 days)
[VERIFIED — Nurseslab, Korea Clinic Guide, U Washington, LVS Clinic]

**ABSOLUTELY NO:**
- Speaking (not even whispering — whisper strains MORE than soft voice)
- Laughing (forces air through healing folds)
- Coughing (slams folds together — can disrupt the new web)
- Throat-clearing (same as cough)
- Sneezing (if you feel one coming, hold your nose and let it out your mouth gently as a soft "ah")
- Singing, humming, lip trills, ANY vocalization
- Mouthing words silently (still engages voice muscles)
- Heavy lifting (raises blood pressure in the neck)
- Smoking, alcohol (delays healing)

**What you CAN do:**
- Breathe normally
- Swallow (carefully at first; some throat soreness)
- Eat soft foods (avoid spicy, hot, scratchy foods)
- Hydrate heavily (water, herbal tea, no caffeine)
- Communicate via text, writing, sign language, text-to-speech apps

**How it feels:** Boring. Isolating. Frustrating. Many report a "cabin fever" effect by day 7-10. Plan ahead — podcasts, audiobooks, TV, journaling.

### Days 14-21 (or 21-28 if extended rest): VERY GENTLE reintroduction
[VERIFIED — LVS Clinic recommends voice therapy starts at week 3]

- First SOFT phonation on day 14 (or whenever surgeon clears)
- 5-10 minutes of soft humming, soft "ah", gentle vowel sounds
- Sensation: voice sounds different IMMEDIATELY — usually higher pitched but also breathy and weak (this is NORMAL — the surgical site is still healing)
- DO NOT push for volume, range, or clarity yet
- Daily voice use: 5-15 minutes total, soft only

### Weeks 3-6: Structured voice therapy begins
- Voice therapy sessions 1-2x per week with a gender-affirming SLP
- Gentle exercises: humming, lip trills, sirens on soft pitches
- Pitch awareness: getting used to the new baseline
- Avoid: forced coughing, shouting, sustained loud phonation
- Daily voice use: 30-60 min total, still soft-to-normal volume only

### Weeks 6-12: Building strength and flexibility
- Therapy continues 1x weekly
- Gradual return to normal volume
- Begin resonance work (surgery did NOT change your mouth/throat shape — the resonance work from B2/B7 still applies)
- Begin intonation work (surgery did NOT change your sentence-melody habits)
- Begin articulation work
- Daily voice use: normal, with rest breaks

### Months 3-6: Integration
- Therapy continues, may taper to biweekly
- The surgical web is now stable
- Voice should sound noticeably higher than pre-op baseline
- Pitch gain typically peaks around months 3-6
- Daily voice use: fully normal, with continued technique practice

### Months 6-12: Long-term stabilization
- Final pitch and quality outcome is clear by month 6
- Some continued improvement through month 12
- Surgery + therapy typically yields +30-60 Hz speaking fundamental frequency gain (some studies report +20 to +81 Hz range across patients)
- Daily voice use: normal; technique maintenance practice ongoing

## SECTION 4 — POST-OP VOICE THERAPY PROTOCOL (what the learner actually does)

### Week 3-4: Finding the new voice (gentle)

**Daily 10-min routine:**
1. Soft hum on comfortable pitch ×10
2. Soft "ah" on comfortable pitch ×10 (5 sec each)
3. Gentle lip trill on comfortable pitch ×5
4. Soft siren, SMALL range (no big glides yet) ×5
5. Rest

**Sensations:**
- Voice sounds weak, breathy, and unstable — this is NORMAL. The web is fresh; folds are learning to vibrate in the new configuration.
- Some throat discomfort, like a mild sore throat, is normal for weeks
- Pitch should feel EASIER in the upper range than before

### Week 5-8: Range and resonance

**Daily 15-min routine:**
1. Warmup (week 3-4 routine, 5 min)
2. Gentle sirens, slightly larger range ×5
3. Soft vowels: "ah" "ee" "oo" on comfortable pitches ×10 each
4. Sound-first words from B8d (single syllables: "we", "no", "see") ×10
5. Light reading aloud, soft volume, 2-3 minutes
6. Rest

**Adding B7 elements:**
- Begin posture cues (tongue forward, slight smile, jaw open)
- Begin forward-buzz awareness
- Begin breathy edge (CAUTIOUSLY — the surgical site is still healing)

### Week 9-12: Quality integration

**Daily 20-min routine:**
1. Warmup (10 min)
2. Sirens across fuller range
3. Sustained vowels in mix voice
4. Short phrases from B8d bridging work
5. Reading aloud 3-5 min
6. Light conversational practice with trusted partner
7. Cooldown

### Months 4-12: Carryover
- Resume the full B8d Master Bridge Protocol
- The carryover problem (sound-isolation → spontaneous speech) STILL APPLIES — surgery changes the source but not the speech habits
- All the resonance, intonation, articulation, and prosody work from B5/B7 is still required

## SECTION 5 — PITCH OUTCOMES (what to expect)

[VERIFIED — multiple peer-reviewed studies]

### Typical pitch gain from Wendler glottoplasty + post-op therapy:
- Pre-op average speaking F0: ~110-130 Hz (male-typical range)
- Post-op average speaking F0: ~160-200 Hz (often crosses into female-typical range of 165-255 Hz)
- **Average gain: +30-60 Hz** (range +20 to +81 Hz across studies)
- Younger patients tend to have better outcomes (Wendler 2013)
- Combination surgery + therapy beats therapy alone (García M., et al. Laryngoscope 2021)
- The biggest gain comes in the first 3 months; smaller gains through month 12

### What surgery DOES NOT change:
- Resonance (vocal tract shape) — still needs B2/B7 work
- Intonation (sentence melody) — still needs B5 work
- Articulation (/s/ brightness, etc.) — still needs B5 work
- Non-speech vocalizations (laugh, cough, cry) — still needs B10 work
- Voice part in choir — may change, may not, depends on starting range

### Who benefits most:
- Pre-op speaking pitch <140 Hz (those with most "work to do")
- Younger patients
- Patients who completed pre-op therapy (better post-op outcomes)
- Patients who complete post-op therapy (essential)

## SECTION 6 — COMPLICATIONS & WARNING SIGNS

[VERIFIED — Optecoto 2023 review, AAO-HNS Bulletin, LVS Clinic]

### Web dehiscence (the web comes apart)
- **What it is:** The sutures or scarring at the front of the folds fail to hold; the web partially or fully breaks down
- **When it happens:** Usually within the first 2-4 weeks (often from coughing, throat-clearing, or voice strain during the rest period)
- **Signs:** Sudden drop in pitch back toward pre-op levels; new hoarseness; voice sounds "broken"
- **What to do:** STOP all voice use, contact surgeon immediately
- **Outcome:** May require revision surgery; some loss of pitch gain permanent

### Granuloma formation
- **What it is:** A benign growth of inflammatory tissue at the surgical site
- **Signs:** Persistent throat pain, persistent hoarseness beyond expected timeline, feeling of "something stuck" in throat
- **Treatment:** Often resolves with voice rest + steroids; may need surgical removal
- **Onset:** Weeks to months post-op

### Pitch instability
- **What it is:** Pitch varies unpredictably, voice "breaks"
- **Signs:** Voice cracks, pitch jumps or drops unexpectedly
- **Treatment:** Voice therapy to stabilize; usually resolves over months

### Impaired projection / reduced loudness
- **What it is:** Voice cannot get loud without strain
- **Signs:** Cannot be heard in noise; voice tires quickly in loud environments
- **Cause:** The shortened folds cannot generate as much acoustic power
- **Outcome:** Often permanent; patients learn to use amplification or accept quieter voice

### Worsened vocal quality
- **What it is:** Voice sounds worse than pre-op (more breathy, more strained, less clear)
- **Signs:** Persistent hoarseness, breathiness that doesn't improve, vocal fatigue
- **Outcome:** Sometimes permanent; revision surgery may help; sometimes the original voice cannot be restored

### Other complications
- Infection (rare with endoscopic technique; treated with antibiotics)
- Bleeding (rare; usually self-limited)
- Suture spitting (sutures work their way out; minor; surgeon removes)
- Persistent throat discomfort (weeks to months; usually resolves)
- Swallowing difficulties (usually transient, first few days)

## SECTION 7 — RED FLAGS — CONTACT SURGEON IMMEDIATELY IF...

[VERIFIED — clinical consensus]

- Sudden pitch drop in first 4 weeks (possible web dehiscence)
- Severe throat pain not controlled by pain meds
- Bleeding from mouth or nose
- Difficulty breathing
- Difficulty swallowing that worsens (not the expected mild first-few-days soreness)
- Fever (>101°F / >38.3°C)
- Voice that suddenly disappears
- Coughing up blood

## SECTION 8 — PRE-EXISTING VOCAL PATHOLOGY (special cases)

If the learner had pre-existing voice issues before surgery:
- **Pre-existing nodules:** Surgery may be more complicated; discuss with surgeon
- **Pre-existing MTD (muscle tension dysphonia):** Therapy is essential before AND after surgery; MTD can sabotage healing
- **Pre-existing reflux (LPR):** Must be controlled before surgery (acid on healing tissue = bad outcome)
- **Pre-existing allergies/asthma:** Manage aggressively during recovery (coughing is the enemy of healing)

## SECTION 9 — THE POST-OP MIND GAME

[VERIFIED — patient accounts across community sources]

### Common psychological experiences
- **Voice grief:** Mourning the loss of the old voice even though you wanted to change it
- **Voice dysphoria spikes:** Hearing the new (often weak/breathy/unstable) voice and panicking that surgery was a mistake
- **Impatience:** Wanting the final result NOW; the recovery is slow (6-12 months to final outcome)
- **Isolation:** 2 weeks of voice rest is socially isolating
- **Comparison:** Other patients' results look/sound better/different — every voice is different
- **Disappointment:** Some patients get less pitch gain than hoped; some need revision surgery

### What helps
- Pre-op counseling about realistic outcomes
- A support system that knows about the voice rest period
- A gender-affirming therapist (separate from voice therapy) for the emotional work
- Connecting with other post-op trans women (online communities)
- Recording your voice monthly to track gradual progress (you won't notice day to day)
- Patience — the final outcome is months away, not days

## SECTION 10 — DECISION SUPPORT (one paragraph)

Voice feminization surgery is ONE option. Pre-op voice therapy alone yields significant gains for many trans women (sometimes enough to satisfy — see B6 evidence). Surgery is for those who completed therapy, achieved what therapy can offer, and still want more pitch elevation. The decision trades pitch gain for vocal power/flexibility/range (see B9 §13 for singers specifically). The decision is irreversible (web cannot be easily undone) and personal. Get multiple surgeon consultations; ask specifically about THEIR complication rates and average pitch gains; ask to speak to former patients. The cheapest surgeon is rarely the best surgeon. Recovery is months, not weeks. Post-op therapy is essential, not optional.
